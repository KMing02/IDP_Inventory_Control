End-to-End Inventory Control via Monotonic Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks
What a certified monotonicity guarantee does, and what it costs
AuthorsQi Zhang, Keming Lin
ExaminerProf. Dr. Stefan Minner
Munich, October 2026

Abstract

Neural inventory policies trained end to end through a simulator can learn the ordering decision directly from data, but nothing prevents them from learning decisions a manager would reject, such as ordering more because more stock is already on hand. Such common-sense rules are monotonicity properties, and MonoKAN, a Kolmogorov–Arnold network whose spline edges are projected onto monotone shapes, guarantees them by construction. We study what this guarantee does and what it costs in the lost-sales setting of Maggiar et al. (2025), always against an unconstrained network of the same architecture, trained and evaluated under the same protocol.

In our main design the network decides the order quantity itself from 37 inputs and the guarantee covers all of them. Without it, the same KAN orders more as stock rises on {{BREAKS[0]|.1f}}% to {{BREAKS[1]|.1f}}% of products in each of six training runs; with it, never. The profit comparison depends on how the networks are initialised, so we make it from identical starts. From the start that worked better for both, the certified model earned {{-DP['constant']['mean']|.2f}} less profit per period than the unconstrained KAN (95% CI {{-DP['constant']['ci'][1]|.2f}} to {{-DP['constant']['ci'][0]|.2f}}, about {{cost_share|.1f}}% of profit). From the other start, the unconstrained KAN barely learned to react to stock, and the certified model earned {{DP['open']['mean']|.0f}} more. The guarantee does not address a second weakness shared by all direct-quantity networks: they lower their order by at most {{max_reaction|.2f}} units per unit of stock, against {{-R.OMNISCIENT_SLOPE|.2f}} for the optimal policy. In an alternative design that orders up to a learned quantile of observed demand, the structure itself enforces the stock rule and the guarantee only covers the economic inputs. There we detected no significant difference in mean profit in our final setup ({{TP['expanding']['mean']|.2f}} per period, 95% CI {{TP['expanding']['ci'][0]|.2f}} to {{TP['expanding']['ci'][1]|.2f}}), and in a smaller setup matched to the main design the certified model earned {{TP['matched']['mean']|.2f}} more per period. The final alternative policy reaches {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret against the omniscient benchmark, competitive with but not better than classical rules given the same information. These results are consistent with a guarantee whose cost grows with the work it has to do, although the two designs differ in more than the constraint. The alternative design's advantage rests on knowing that the optimal policy is order-up-to, a route Maggiar et al. themselves call fragile.

1 Introduction

Every day a retailer decides how many units of each product to order. Too many, and the surplus ties up money and shelf space; too few, and customers leave empty-handed. The classical approach estimates the demand distribution first and then derives the order from it. End-to-end learning skips the separate forecast: a neural network observes the state of the system, including the stock on hand, recent demand and the product's prices and costs, and outputs the order directly, trained to maximise simulated profit. Maggiar et al. (2025) show that such a policy, trained on synthetic products with known optimal benchmarks, comes within {{R.PAPER_TABLE3_GAP|.2f}}% of the omniscient benchmark in average profit.

A network that learns the whole decision also learns behaviour nobody checked. Nothing in its training stops it from ordering more when more stock is already on hand, or less when the selling price rises. Each such decision may be rare and small, but a manager cannot verify a black box input by input, and a single absurd recommendation is enough to lose trust in a system. These common-sense rules share a mathematical form: when one input rises, the order should move in only one direction. This is monotonicity, and it can be guaranteed by the architecture rather than hoped for after training, a long-standing idea in machine learning (Sill, 1998; You et al., 2017). MonoKAN (Polo-Molina et al., 2024) is such an architecture: a Kolmogorov–Arnold network (Liu et al., 2024) whose learned edge functions are constrained to be monotone, which certifies the direction of every designated input for all possible inputs.

The research question of this project is therefore: what does a certified monotonicity guarantee cost in an end-to-end inventory policy, and where does it actually matter? We answer it by comparing MonoKAN with an unconstrained KAN of the same architecture, trained and evaluated under the same protocol and started from the same initialisation. In the main design the start turned out to matter, so there we compare the two networks from each of two starts (Section 4.1).

Following the examiner's feedback on our intermediate results, the main design of this report lets the network decide the order quantity itself. In this design the rule "more stock, never a larger order" has to come from the network, so the guarantee does real work. We also report an alternative design, which orders up to a learned target stock. There the structure of the policy already enforces the stock rule, and the guarantee only covers the economic inputs. Comparing both designs shows how the effect of the guarantee differs between them.

The contributions are:

  • a direct-quantity MonoKAN policy that certifies the direction of all 37 inputs, and a matched comparison with the same KAN without the certificate and with the feed-forward network of Maggiar et al.;
  • a measurement of how often the unconstrained policy breaks the stock rule, and of the profit gap between the certified and the unconstrained model when both start from the same initialisation;
  • a soft-quantile target-stock policy with a monotone level network, the steps that took it from {{R.DEVELOPMENT[0][1]|.1f}}% to {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret, and comparisons with classical rules that use the same information;
  • evidence on the practical promises of MonoKAN: interpretability, model size and speed, and data efficiency.

Section 2 describes the setting and Section 3 the certified network. Section 4 introduces both designs and the protocol. Sections 5 and 6 report the results of each design, Section 7 compares them, Section 8 discusses the practical questions and the limitations, and Section 9 concludes.

2 Setting

2.1 The lost-sales inventory problem

We use the synthetic lost-sales setting of Maggiar et al. (2025). Each product has a selling price p, a purchase cost c, a holding cost h per unit left over at the end of a period and a penalty k per unit of unmet demand. Orders arrive immediately (no lead time) and unmet demand is lost. In period t the policy sees the stock on hand It and orders qt ≥ 0; then demand dt is realised, and the period's profit and the next stock are

rt = p·min(dt, It + qt) − c·qt − h·(It + qt − dt)+ − k·(dt − It − qt)+,    It+1 = (It + qt − dt)+.
(1)

Demand is independent and identically distributed over time and follows a Gamma distribution whose mean μ and coefficient of variation ν differ between products. These parameters are hidden: a policy observes only the product's economics and its demand history, which starts with 32 past observations. Products are drawn as in the paper: p exponential with mean 100, c = p·U, h exponential with mean 5, k = 10·U, μ exponential with mean 100 and ν uniform on (0, 1), with independent uniform draws U.

With no lead time and independent demand, the optimal policy is a myopic base-stock policy (Zipkin, 2000): order up to the critical-fractile quantile of the demand distribution,

S* = F−1(β),   β = p − c + kp − c + k + h,   qt = (S* − It)+.
(2)

This is the newsvendor solution. With the true Gamma parameters it defines the omniscient benchmark: a distribution-informed base-stock policy that knows the demand distribution but not the future demand. It is our reference point, not a strict upper bound; on a particular demand path another policy can do as well or better. Classical non-omniscient rules estimate F−1(β) from observed demand, either as an empirical quantile or by fitting a Gamma distribution (Section 4.3).

2.2 Training end to end through the simulator

All learned policies are trained by DirectBackprop, as in the paper: the simulator in (1) is written in a differentiable form, a policy runs a batch of products for a 100-period training horizon, and the gradient of the total profit flows through the simulated dynamics into the network's parameters. Each training product has a fixed future demand path, and leftover stock at the end of the horizon is credited at its purchase cost, so that ending the horizon with stock is not penalised. We train with Adam at learning rate 0.001, select the checkpoint with the best validation profit, and repeat every experiment with three seeds (2025, 2026, 2027).

2.3 Measuring performance

Every policy is evaluated on the same 300 validation products and the same demand paths, over 520 periods of which the first 20 are discarded so that the starting stock does not matter. We report two measures.

Profit per period, averaged over products, is the paper's measure. Product economics are heavy tailed (profit per period has mean {{R.PRODUCT_REWARD_MEAN|,}} and standard deviation {{R.PRODUCT_REWARD_SD|,}} over products), so this average is dominated by a few large products.

Regret is the mean per-product shortfall against the omniscient policy, in percent:

regret = 100|𝒫+| ∑i ∈ 𝒫+ Ri* − RiRi*,
(3)

where Ri and Ri* are the profits of the policy and the omniscient policy on product i. Every product counts the same, so regret reveals failures on typical products that the average hides. The set 𝒫+ excludes the 21 of 300 products on which even the omniscient policy makes no profit, where a relative shortfall is meaningless. The two measures can disagree sharply: our first MonoKAN design trailed a classical rule by only 3.6% in average profit, yet its regret was 21.4%: a typical product lost a fifth of the profit it could have made.

Because all policies face identical products and demand, we compare them paired by product, which removes the large product-to-product spread. For a difference in profit we first average each product's profit over the three training seeds and then compare product by product. We report the mean difference with its 95% confidence interval and the paired t-test, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which is less sensitive to a few very large products, and the share of products on which one policy is ahead. These tests treat the trained models as given: they describe variation across products, not the variation from retraining with new seeds, which three seeds can only indicate.

3 Certified Monotonicity with MonoKAN

3.1 Kolmogorov–Arnold networks

A multilayer perceptron applies fixed nonlinearities at its nodes and learns linear weights on its edges. A Kolmogorov–Arnold network (KAN) does the reverse: every edge carries its own learnable univariate function, and nodes only sum their incoming edges (Liu et al., 2024). A layer maps x ∈ ℝn to

yj = ∑i φji(xi),   φji(x) = wbji·b(x) + wsji·sji(x),
(4)

where b is a fixed increasing base function (a shifted, positive ELU in our implementation) and sji is a learned cubic Hermite spline on a grid of knots, with the value and slope at each knot as parameters. Because each edge is a curve of one variable, a trained KAN can be inspected edge by edge.

3.2 Monotone splines and the certificate

A cubic Hermite spline is monotone if its knot values are monotone and its knot slopes satisfy the condition of Fritsch and Carlson (1980): with Δ the secant slope of an interval and α, β the two knot slopes divided by Δ, the interval is monotone when α, β ≥ 0 and α² + β² ≤ 9. MonoKAN (Polo-Molina et al., 2024) uses this to certify monotonicity. After every optimiser step we project the parameters back onto the monotone set:

  • for every input with a required direction, the first-layer edges get a base weight of that sign, a non-negative spline weight, and a spline projected to be monotone in that direction (knot values made monotone, knot slopes given the right sign and scaled until the Fritsch–Carlson condition holds);
  • every edge in later layers is projected to be increasing, with non-negative weights.

A sum of functions that move in one direction moves in that direction, and an increasing function of it keeps it. The network's output is therefore monotone in every designated input, with the designated sign, for every possible input, not only on the training data, up to floating-point rounding. This is a property of the architecture, not a statistical finding.

3.3 Two implementation lessons

Two defects in our first implementation are worth recording, because both changed results. First, the projection wrote the conditions of neighbouring intervals one after the other, although adjacent intervals share a knot slope. The second write could undo the first, leaving rare, tiny violations (worst case 5.6·10−4). The corrected projection combines both conditions at each knot, and a regression test checks it on uneven grids. Second, every spline originally lived on a fixed grid from −2 to 4, while the normalised inputs did not. 63.8% of demand observations fell into the first of seven intervals, and holding cost and lost-sale penalty spanned less than one interval. Those splines could barely bend, and the first model learned an almost constant policy. Placing the knots at quantiles of the training data, the spline analogue of the grid update of Liu et al. (2024), more than halved the regret of our first design (Section 6.1). Knot placement never changes the sign of a slope, so the certificate is unaffected.

4 Two Policy Designs

4.1 Main design: the network decides the order

The main design maps everything a manager would look at to the order quantity. The inputs are the stock on hand, the demand of the last 32 periods and the four economic quantities: 1 + 32 + 4 = 37 inputs. We use 32 periods because the paper's setting provides 32 periods of demand history. With orders arriving immediately there is no pipeline stock, so no further state is needed. The rule for each input is fixed in advance (Table 1), and MonoKAN certifies all 37. The demand inputs are sorted before entering the network. Each sorted value is nondecreasing in every raw observation, so the certified directions carry over to the raw inputs. The output passes through a softplus and is multiplied by the mean of the 32 observations available at the start, which makes the order non-negative and proportional to the product's scale without changing any direction. In evaluation, but not in training, each order is also capped so that stock after ordering does not exceed four times that mean. The cap falls with stock and rises with that mean, so it keeps every certified direction, and it applies to all arms alike; Section 5.2 reports its effect.

MonoKAN uses one hidden layer of 8 nodes and Hermite splines with 5 intervals placed at data quantiles, {{D['monokan']['params']|,}} parameters in total. The comparison KAN has the same architecture and training but no projection. Either network can start from one of two initialisations of its output layer. In the constant start the output-layer weights are zero, so every product starts at the same order per unit of mean demand: the median empirical newsvendor level of the training products. In the open start the weights are nonzero, so the initial order already varies with the inputs, and the bias is shifted so that the median initial order on reference contexts equals that level. Our first runs started MonoKAN open and the KAN constant. Because the start turned out to matter, we trained both networks from both starts and compare them from identical starts (Section 5.2). The third arm is the feed-forward network (FCNN) of Maggiar et al. with {{D['fcnn']['params']|,}} parameters and its own initialisation. All arms are trained on the same 1,000 products for 2,400 epochs to maximise total profit.

To check the stock rule we sweep the stock on hand of every validation product from 0 to 4 days of mean demand in 25 steps, holding all other inputs at the product's observed values, and flag every step at which the order rises (by more than 10−4 of a day's demand). The slope of the order against stock over the first 1.5 days measures how strongly a policy reacts. An order-up-to policy has slope −1 until it stops ordering; the omniscient policy averages {{R.OMNISCIENT_SLOPE|.2f}} on this sweep.

4.2 Alternative design: order up to a learned target

Equation (2) suggests a different structure: let the network choose only the percentile, and let a fixed formula turn it into an order. A small MonoKAN level network reads only the four economic inputs and outputs a percentile u = σ(f(p, c, h, k)). The target stock is a smoothed empirical quantile of all demand observed so far. With z(1) ≤ … ≤ z(n) the sorted observations and tj = (j − 1)/(n − 1) their positions,

St = g(u) · [ z(1) + ∑j=2n σ((u − tj)/τ) · (z(j) − z(j−1)) ],   qt = (St − It)+,
(5)

where the sigmoid weights select the order statistics around position u, the width τ is learned, and g(u) = exp(a·u + b) with a ≥ 0 is a learned gain that increases with the level. Every term moves the right way. The weights wj = σ((u − tj)/τ) lie in (0, 1) and decrease in j, because the positions tj increase. Rearranged, the bracket in (5) is (1 − w2) z(1) + ∑j=2n−1 (wj − wj+1) z(j) + wn z(n), a combination of the order statistics with non-negative coefficients; each order statistic is nondecreasing in every observed demand, and the positive gain preserves this, so St is nondecreasing in every observed demand. St increases with u, which MonoKAN makes increase in price and penalty and decrease in purchase and holding cost. And the subtraction makes the order nonincreasing in stock for any level network. The certificate therefore only has to cover four inputs, which is exactly why this design is a useful contrast. The level network has two layers of 8 nodes and 7-interval splines, {{R.LEVEL_PARAMS|,}} parameters. The final policy is trained on 5,000 products with a scaled objective: each product's simulated profit is divided by pi · μ̂i · T, its price times its observed mean demand times the horizon (each floored at 1), before averaging over products, so that a few large products do not dominate the gradient.

The same decoder can use three kinds of demand information, which matters for fair comparisons: one fixed estimate from the first 32 days, the last 32 days updated every period, or all demand seen so far (the final policy). Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.1) suggest such order-up-to structures, but warn that they are fragile: they only work when the optimal policy is known to be of this form.

4.3 Baselines and the matched protocol

Besides the omniscient policy, we use two classical rules that estimate the target from observed demand: the empirical quantile at the critical fractile, and a Gamma distribution fitted by the method of moments. The Gamma fit is told that demand is Gamma distributed, an advantage no learned policy has. A comparison is only fair if both sides see the same data, so each rule is evaluated with each of the three kinds of information:

  • first 32 days, never updated: how the paper defines its non-omniscient benchmarks;
  • last 32 days, updated daily: what a policy in daily use sees, and the demand window of the main design's inputs;
  • all demand seen so far: the information of the final alternative policy.

Within each comparison, every arm shares the products, demand paths, seeds, horizon, simulator, reward and training budget; for MonoKAN against KAN it also shares the architecture, the parameter count and the initialisation (except in the first main-design runs, which Section 5.2 reports separately). Only the factor under study changes.

5 Results: Main Design

In short. Without the certificate the same KAN breaks the stock rule in every training run, on {{BREAKS[0]|.0f}}% to {{BREAKS[1]|.0f}}% of the products. The certificate removes this completely. 5.1 Without the certificate, the stock rule breaks

From the constant start, the KAN without the certificate orders more as stock rises on {{D['kan']['violation']|.1f}}% of products on average (Figure 2a). This happens in every training run, but how often varies widely: {{KS[0]|.1f}}%, {{KS[1]|.1f}}% and {{KS[2]|.1f}}% of products for the three seeds. The breaks occur at {{D['kan']['violating_steps']|.1f}}% of all swept stock steps; most are small, but the largest is an extra order of about {{D['kan']['worst_rise']|.0f}}% of a day's demand. From the open start the KAN breaks the rule on {{KO[0]|.1f}}%, {{KO[1]|.1f}}% and {{KO[2]|.1f}}% of products, but these breaks are tiny, at most {{D['kan_open']['worst_rise']|.1f}}% of a day's demand, because this KAN barely reacts to stock at all (Section 5.2). Figure 2b shows the largest reversal we found in any run; the break rates above show how common reversals are. Over part of the stock range, the KAN's order goes up as stock goes up. The order of the certified MonoKAN on the same product never rises with stock. MonoKAN breaks the rule for no start, seed or product, as its construction guarantees.

The risk depends on the model family. The paper's FCNN did not break the rule in any of our three runs, while the KAN did in all six runs, from either start. A practitioner cannot know in advance which kind of model and which training run they will get. The certificate removes that uncertainty: no input combination, seen or unseen, can produce a larger order from more stock. Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.3) instead add a soft penalty for violations to the training loss, which makes breaks less likely but cannot exclude them.

5.3 What the certificate cannot fix

Monotonicity fixes the direction of a response, not its strength. An order-up-to policy lowers its order one unit for every unit of stock until it stops ordering, which gives {{-R.OMNISCIENT_SLOPE|.2f}} on our sweep. Every network reacts far more weakly, at most {{max_reaction|.2f}} (Figure 3), so the networks keep ordering although enough stock is on hand. This likely explains much of their high regret, and the certificate cannot address it. Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.2) name this as one reason to set monotonic networks aside: they fix the sign of a reaction but not its size. Our results are consistent with this concern.

No direct-quantity network comes close to a classical rule that uses the same 32-day window. The empirical quantile and the Gamma fit on the last 32 days reach {{C['percentile']['rolling'][1]|.2f}}% and {{C['gamma']['rolling'][1]|.2f}}% regret, against {{D['kan']['regret']|.2f}}% or more for every network. The paper's interpretability test points the same way. A direct-quantity policy's order at zero stock is its implied order-up-to level. Per unit of mean demand, which removes product scale, MonoKAN's implied level correlates only {{IC['monokan_constant']|.2f}} to {{IC['monokan']|.2f}} with the optimal level, depending on the start, and the unconstrained KAN's {{IC['kan']|.2f}} from the constant start, against {{R.CLASSICAL_IMPLIED_CORR|.2f}} for the Gamma fit on the same history. In raw units every policy looks good, because product scale alone explains most of the variation. The certificate makes the direction of every input transparent, but the learned levels are only loosely tied to the optimum.

6 Results: Alternative Design

In short. With the stock rule built into the structure, the final policy reaches {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret with {{R.LEVEL_PARAMS|,}} parameters and no rule violations. In every information window it has a lower regret than the same KAN without the certificate. Against classical rules with the same information it is competitive, but not better.

6.1 From {{R.DEVELOPMENT[0][1]|.1f}}% to {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret

The first MonoKAN in this design, a deep network that output the target stock directly, reached {{R.DEVELOPMENT[0][1]|.2f}}% regret, worse than an unconstrained FCNN in the same structure ({{R.FIRST_FCNN_REGRET|.2f}}%) and about twelve times the regret of a classical rule. An unconstrained KAN of the same architecture was similarly poor (20.68%), so the constraint was not the main cause. Figure 4 shows the steps that fixed it, each applied cumulatively. All variants were designed to enforce monotonicity; the projection defect of Section 3.3 was corrected at the fourth step. The two largest gains came from placing the spline knots where the data lies (Section 3.3) and from the soft-quantile structure of equation (5). Using all demand seen so far, weighting every product equally in the loss and training on more products brought the regret to {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}%. On 3,000 validation products the final policy reaches {{R.FINAL_REGRET_3000|.2f}}%.

Several plausible changes did not help (Appendix C). Making the KAN larger made it worse. A warm start that imitated the classical rule before profit training was severely harmful, because the static rule it imitated differs from the expanding-window policy that is deployed. A longer training horizon did not help either.

6.2 Comparison at matched information

A learned policy that uses all demand seen so far must not be compared with a rule that uses only the first 32 days; that would credit the policy with information, not skill. Table 4 and Figure 5 therefore compare every policy within each kind of information. Two patterns stand out. More data helps every policy: for all four, the last column is the best. And within every column the classical Gamma fit is best, although it also knows the demand family. 6.4 Interpretability, speed and data needs

With only four inputs the level network is easy to read (Figure 6a). The learned target stock rises with price and penalty and falls with purchase and holding cost, as certified. The learned percentile u also tracks the newsvendor fractile β of equation (2) closely, with correlation {{R.NEWSVENDOR_CORR|.3f}} (Figure 6b), although the fractile was never given to the model as an input, an initial value or a training target. This shows agreement in the level, not that the policy equals the textbook one: the quantile-based, order-up-to structure was supplied by design, and the decoder has its own learned smoothing and gain.

The final policy has {{R.LEVEL_PARAMS|,}} parameters, against 15,297 for the first design. It is still slower per decision than a feed-forward network of the same size ({{R.TS_MS['monokan']|.2f}} ms against {{R.TS_MS['mlp']|.2f}} ms on a CPU), because spline evaluation is more expensive than a matrix product. For daily ordering decisions this does not matter. MonoKAN also does not need less data. With 100 training products it is worse than a parameter-matched MLP (Figure 7). Across the three tested training-set sizes (three seeds each), MonoKAN's regret fell steadily, while the MLP's rose again at 1,000 products; other sizes, budgets or initialisations may behave differently.

The observed effect of the certificate differs between the two designs. In the main design, where it alone enforces the stock rule and the unconstrained network breaks that rule in every run, the certified model earned slightly less from the start that worked better for both networks, and far more from the start at which the unconstrained network failed. In the alternative, where the subtraction enforces the rule, the certified model earned more in this setup, and in the final setup we detected no difference. This is consistent with a guarantee that costs more the more work it has to do, but the comparison does not isolate the stock constraint: the designs also differ in inputs, architecture and decoder. Removing only the stock constraint from the main design would test the idea directly. With the same data and training budget, the alternative's regret is a fraction of the main design's, so the difference does not come from information but from the policy structure and what comes with it. Building inventory knowledge into the policy helped more than anything else we tried; with all demand seen so far, the matched alternative reaches {{R.MATCHED_EXPANDING_REGRET|.2f}}%.

Why then is the alternative not the main design? Its advantage rests on knowing the shape of the answer in advance. In our setting the optimal policy is order-up-to (equation 2), so a structure that can only produce order-up-to policies loses nothing in principle. Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.1) call this route fragile, and their own experiments show why. With delivery lead times of 2 to 7 periods, where order-up-to is no longer optimal, order-up-to policies fall {{R.PAPER_LEAD_TIME_GAP[0]|.2f}}% to {{R.PAPER_LEAD_TIME_GAP[1]|.2f}}% behind a network that decides the quantity (their Table 4). The direct-quantity design does not impose an order-up-to decoder, although it still makes assumptions: the certified directions, sorted demand inputs, output scaling and limited capacity. It is the more general formulation and the one in which the guarantee has to do the work; we have not verified that it performs better on other inventory problems.

8 Discussion

8.1 Why impose monotonicity if profit is the objective?

Adding a constraint can never improve the optimal profit; at best it leaves it unchanged. In our setting the optimal policy of equation (2) already orders less when stock rises, so at the optimum the stock rule costs nothing. If the optimum could be found exactly and the evaluation products matched the situations met in practice, monotonicity would add nothing to profit. Its role lies elsewhere.

  • Learned policies are approximations. The optimal policy never orders more when stock rises, yet the unconstrained KAN did so in every training run, on {{BREAKS[0]|.0f}}% to {{BREAKS[1]|.0f}}% of products. Training on finite data does not by itself recover the known structure of the optimum.
  • Average profit does not measure every decision. Profit on the evaluation products says little about states that are rare or absent in training. The certificate holds for every input, which is what lets a planner rely on the direction of each recommendation without checking it case by case.
  • Prior knowledge can help or hurt. As a restriction it helped in the smaller matched setup of Section 6.3 and kept training reliable from a start at which the unconstrained KAN failed (Section 5.2); where it had to enforce many directions at once, it cost about {{cost_share|.1f}}% of profit when both networks started well.

We therefore treat monotonicity as a requirement set by the decision maker, comparable to a service-level constraint, and ask what meeting it costs. If profit on the evaluation products were the only criterion, the classical rule would be preferred in the main design, and among the networks the unconstrained KAN, provided it starts well.

8.3 Limitations

  • A simple setting. Demand is stationary, orders arrive immediately and products are synthetic. This is exactly the setting in which order-up-to is optimal, and it favours the alternative design. We have not tested changing demand, lead times or real data.
  • Scale. We train on 1,000 to 5,000 products, compared with 40,000 in the paper, and use three seeds per experiment. Our absolute profit levels are therefore not comparable with the paper's.
  • Selection on the validation products. Checkpoints are selected, and the method was developed, on the same 300 validation products that we report on. Pairing on shared products and demand paths reduces product-level noise, but it does not remove this selection bias. The final alternative policy keeps its standing on 3,000 products ({{R.FINAL_REGRET_3000|.2f}}%), but that set contains the original 300 and is not a fully independent test (Appendix C).
  • Two starts, one evaluation cap. The main design's comparison depends on the initialisation, and we tested two starts; other starts, learning rates or budgets could move the cost of the certificate either way. Its evaluation caps orders, which its training does not (Section 4.1); the cap applies to all arms alike and does not change our conclusions (Section 5.2).
  • The weak stock reaction is unexplained. All direct-quantity networks react to stock about half as strongly as they should. A plausible cause is that the stock levels visited during training cover only a narrow range; we have not verified this.
  • Model family. The stock-rule breaks are a property of the KAN family in our runs. The paper's FCNN did not break the rule, so how often an unconstrained model breaks it depends on the model.
  • The main design's training setup has not yet received the improvements that helped the alternative (5,000 products, per-product weighting), so its absolute performance is likely understated.

9 Conclusion

We asked what a certified monotonicity guarantee costs in an end-to-end inventory policy and where it matters. When the network decides the order itself, the guarantee does real work. Without it, the same network orders more as stock rises on {{BREAKS[0]|.0f}}% to {{BREAKS[1]|.0f}}% of the products, in every training run. With MonoKAN this cannot happen. Started from the same initialisation, the certified model earned about {{cost_share|.1f}}% less when the unconstrained network trained well, and far more from a start at which the unconstrained network barely learned to react to stock. When the structure of the policy already enforces the stock rule, we detected no profit cost of the guarantee, and in a smaller setup the certified model did better. This is consistent with a guarantee whose cost grows with the work it has to do, but our comparison of the designs does not isolate that effect. Building inventory knowledge into the structure produced the best policy of the project, at {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret; this relies on the shape of the optimal policy being known in advance. The guarantee fixes the direction of every response but not its strength, and in this simple setting a well-chosen classical rule with the same information remains better than both designs.

Three directions follow. First, the main design should receive the improvements that helped the alternative: more training products and per-product weighting. Second, removing only the stock constraint from the main design, while keeping the other 36 directions, would show how much of the remaining cost the stock rule itself causes. Further shape restrictions, such as an order that falls by at most one unit per unit of stock, can also be certified; they bound the reaction between −1 and 0, which the current networks already satisfy, so whether they, or training on a wider range of stock levels, make the reaction stronger remains to be tested. Third, settings with changing demand and lead times, where order-up-to policies are no longer optimal, are where a certified direct-quantity policy should be tested next.
