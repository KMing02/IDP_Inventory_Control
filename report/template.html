End-to-End Inventory Control via Monotonic Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks
Where a certified monotonicity guarantee pays, and what it costs
AuthorsQi Zhang, Keming Lin
ExaminerProf. Dr. Stefan Minner
Munich, October 2026

Abstract

Neural inventory policies trained end to end through a simulator can learn the ordering decision directly from data, but nothing prevents them from learning decisions a manager would reject, such as ordering more because more stock is already on hand. Such common-sense rules are monotonicity properties, and MonoKAN, a Kolmogorov–Arnold network whose spline edges are projected onto monotone shapes, guarantees them by construction. We study what this guarantee does and what it costs in the lost-sales setting of Maggiar et al. (2025), always against the identical network without the guarantee, trained and evaluated in exactly the same way.

In our main design the network decides the order quantity itself from 37 inputs and the guarantee covers all of them. Without it, the otherwise identical KAN orders more as stock rises on {{D['kan']['violation']|.1f}}% of products, in every training run; with it, never. The guarantee costs {{-DP['mean']|.2f}} profit per period against that KAN (95% CI {{-DP['ci'][1]|.2f}} to {{-DP['ci'][0]|.2f}}, about {{cost_share|.1f}}% of profit), and it cannot fix a second weakness shared by all direct-quantity networks: they react to stock only about half as strongly as the optimal policy. In an alternative design that orders up to a learned quantile of observed demand, the structure itself enforces the stock rule and the guarantee only covers the economic inputs. There it costs nothing in our final setup and adds {{TP['matched']['mean']|.2f}} profit per period in a smaller setup matched to the main design; the final alternative policy reaches {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret against the omniscient optimum, competitive with but not better than classical rules given the same information. The cost of the guarantee therefore scales with the work it has to do. The alternative design's advantage rests on knowing that the optimal policy is order-up-to, a route Maggiar et al. themselves call fragile.

1 Introduction

Every day a retailer decides how many units of each product to order. Too many, and the surplus ties up money and shelf space; too few, and customers leave empty-handed. The classical approach estimates the demand distribution first and then derives the order from it. End-to-end learning skips the separate forecast: a neural network observes the state of the system, including the stock on hand, recent demand and the product's prices and costs, and outputs the order directly, trained to maximise simulated profit. Maggiar et al. (2025) show that such a policy, trained on synthetic products with known optimal benchmarks, comes within {{R.PAPER_TABLE3_GAP|.2f}}% of the omniscient optimum in average profit.

A network that learns the whole decision also learns behaviour nobody checked. Nothing in its training stops it from ordering more when more stock is already on hand, or less when the selling price rises. Each such decision may be rare and small, but a manager cannot verify a black box input by input, and a single absurd recommendation is enough to lose trust in a system. These common-sense rules share a mathematical form: when one input rises, the order should move in only one direction. This is monotonicity, and it can be guaranteed by the architecture rather than hoped for after training, a long-standing idea in machine learning (Sill, 1998; You et al., 2017). MonoKAN (Polo-Molina et al., 2024) is such an architecture: a Kolmogorov–Arnold network (Liu et al., 2024) whose learned edge functions are constrained to be monotone, which certifies the direction of every designated input for all possible inputs.

The research question of this project is therefore: what does a certified monotonicity guarantee cost in an end-to-end inventory policy, and where does it actually matter? We answer it by comparing MonoKAN with the identical network without the guarantee, under exactly the same training and evaluation, so that any difference is due to the guarantee alone.

Following the examiner's feedback on our intermediate results, the main design of this report lets the network decide the order quantity itself. In this design the rule "more stock, never a larger order" has to come from the network, so the guarantee does real work. We also report an alternative design, which orders up to a learned target stock. There the structure of the policy already enforces the stock rule, and the guarantee only covers the economic inputs. Comparing both designs shows where a guarantee pays and where it is nearly free.

The contributions are:

  • a direct-quantity MonoKAN policy that certifies the direction of all 37 inputs, and a matched comparison with the same KAN without the certificate and with the feed-forward network of Maggiar et al.;
  • a measurement of how often the unconstrained policy breaks the stock rule and of what the guarantee costs;
  • a soft-quantile target-stock policy with a monotone level network, the steps that took it from {{R.DEVELOPMENT[0][1]|.1f}}% to {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret, and comparisons with classical rules that use the same information;
  • evidence on the practical promises of MonoKAN: interpretability, model size and speed, and data efficiency.

Section 2 describes the setting and Section 3 the certified network. Section 4 introduces both designs and the protocol. Sections 5 and 6 report the results of each design, Section 7 compares them, Section 8 discusses the practical questions and the limitations, and Section 9 concludes.

2 Setting

2.1 The lost-sales inventory problem

We use the synthetic lost-sales setting of Maggiar et al. (2025). Each product has a selling price p, a purchase cost c, a holding cost h per unit left over at the end of a period and a penalty k per unit of unmet demand. Orders arrive immediately (no lead time) and unmet demand is lost. In period t the policy sees the stock on hand It and orders qt ≥ 0; then demand dt is realised, and the period's profit and the next stock are

rt = p·min(dt, It + qt) − c·qt − h·(It + qt − dt)+ − k·(dt − It − qt)+,    It+1 = (It + qt − dt)+.
(1)

Demand is independent and identically distributed over time and follows a Gamma distribution whose mean μ and coefficient of variation ν differ between products. These parameters are hidden: a policy observes only the product's economics and its demand history, which starts with 32 past observations. Products are drawn as in the paper: p exponential with mean 100, c = p·U, h exponential with mean 5, k = 10·U, μ exponential with mean 100 and ν uniform on (0, 1), with independent uniform draws U.

With no lead time and independent demand, the optimal policy is myopic: order up to the critical-fractile quantile of the demand distribution,

S* = F−1(β),   β = p − c + kp − c + k + h,   qt = (S* − It)+.
(2)

This is the newsvendor solution. With the true Gamma parameters it defines the omniscient benchmark, which no real policy can reach because the parameters are hidden. Classical non-omniscient rules estimate F−1(β) from observed demand, either as an empirical quantile or by fitting a Gamma distribution (Section 4.3).

2.2 Training end to end through the simulator

All learned policies are trained by DirectBackprop, as in the paper: the simulator in (1) is written in a differentiable form, a policy runs a batch of products for a 100-period training horizon, and the gradient of the total profit flows through the simulated dynamics into the network's parameters. Each training product has a fixed future demand path, and leftover stock at the end of the horizon is credited at its purchase cost, so that ending the horizon with stock is not penalised. We train with Adam at learning rate 0.001, select the checkpoint with the best validation profit, and repeat every experiment with three seeds (2025, 2026, 2027).

2.3 Measuring performance

Every policy is evaluated on the same 300 validation products and the same demand paths, over 520 periods of which the first 20 are discarded so that the starting stock does not matter. We report two measures.

Profit per period, averaged over products, is the paper's measure. Product economics are heavy tailed (profit per period has mean {{R.PRODUCT_REWARD_MEAN|,}} and standard deviation {{R.PRODUCT_REWARD_SD|,}} over products), so this average is dominated by a few large products.

Regret is the mean per-product shortfall against the omniscient policy, in percent:

regret = 100|𝒫+| ∑i ∈ 𝒫+ Ri* − RiRi*,
(3)

where Ri and Ri* are the profits of the policy and the omniscient policy on product i. Every product counts the same, so regret reveals failures on typical products that the average hides. The set 𝒫+ excludes the 21 of 300 products on which even the omniscient policy makes no profit, where a relative shortfall is meaningless. The two measures can disagree sharply: our first MonoKAN design trailed a classical rule by only 3.6% in average profit, yet its regret was 21.4%: a typical product lost a fifth of the profit it could have made.

Because all policies face identical products and demand, we compare them paired by product, which removes the large product-to-product spread. For a difference in profit we report the mean with its 95% confidence interval and the paired t-test, which speak about the average, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test together with the share of products on which one policy is ahead, which speak about the typical product.

3 Certified Monotonicity with MonoKAN

3.1 Kolmogorov–Arnold networks

A multilayer perceptron applies fixed nonlinearities at its nodes and learns linear weights on its edges. A Kolmogorov–Arnold network (KAN) does the reverse: every edge carries its own learnable univariate function, and nodes only sum their incoming edges (Liu et al., 2024). A layer maps x ∈ ℝn to

yj = ∑i φji(xi),   φji(x) = wbji·b(x) + wsji·sji(x),
(4)

where b is a fixed increasing base function (a shifted, positive ELU in our implementation) and sji is a learned cubic Hermite spline on a grid of knots, with the value and slope at each knot as parameters. Because each edge is a curve of one variable, a trained KAN can be inspected edge by edge.

3.2 Monotone splines and the certificate

A cubic Hermite spline is monotone if its knot values are monotone and its knot slopes satisfy the condition of Fritsch and Carlson (1980): with Δ the secant slope of an interval and α, β the two knot slopes divided by Δ, the interval is monotone when α, β ≥ 0 and α² + β² ≤ 9. MonoKAN (Polo-Molina et al., 2024) uses this to certify monotonicity. After every optimiser step we project the parameters back onto the monotone set:

  • for every input with a required direction, the first-layer edges get a base weight of that sign, a non-negative spline weight, and a spline projected to be monotone in that direction (knot values made monotone, knot slopes given the right sign and scaled until the Fritsch–Carlson condition holds);
  • every edge in later layers is projected to be increasing, with non-negative weights.

A sum of functions that move in one direction moves in that direction, and an increasing function of it keeps it. The network's output is therefore monotone in every designated input, with the designated sign, for every possible input, not only on the training data. This is a property of the architecture, not a statistical finding.

3.3 Two implementation lessons

Two defects in our first implementation are worth recording, because both changed results. First, the projection wrote the conditions of neighbouring intervals one after the other, although adjacent intervals share a knot slope. The second write could undo the first, leaving rare, tiny violations (worst case 5.6·10−4). The corrected projection combines both conditions at each knot, and a regression test checks it on uneven grids. Second, every spline originally lived on a fixed grid from −2 to 4, while the normalised inputs did not. 63.8% of demand observations fell into the first of seven intervals, and holding cost and lost-sale penalty spanned less than one interval. Those splines could barely bend, and the first model learned an almost constant policy. Placing the knots at quantiles of the training data, the spline analogue of the grid update of Liu et al. (2024), more than halved the regret of our first design (Section 6.1). Knot placement never changes the sign of a slope, so the certificate is unaffected.

4 Two Policy Designs

4.1 Main design: the network decides the order

The main design maps everything a manager would look at to the order quantity. The inputs are the stock on hand, the demand of the last 32 periods and the four economic quantities: 1 + 32 + 4 = 37 inputs. We use 32 periods because the paper's setting provides 32 periods of demand history. With orders arriving immediately there is no pipeline stock, so no further state is needed. The rule for each input is fixed in advance (Table 1), and MonoKAN certifies all 37. The demand inputs are sorted before entering the network. Each sorted value is nondecreasing in every raw observation, so the certified directions carry over to the raw inputs. The output passes through a scaled sigmoid and is multiplied by the mean observed demand, which makes the order non-negative and proportional to the product's scale without changing any direction.

To check the stock rule we sweep the stock on hand of every validation product from 0 to 4 days of mean demand in 25 steps, holding all other inputs at the product's observed values, and flag every step at which the order rises (by more than 10−4 of a day's demand). The slope of the order against stock over the first 1.5 days measures how strongly a policy reacts. An order-up-to policy has slope −1 until it stops ordering; the omniscient policy averages {{R.OMNISCIENT_SLOPE|.2f}} on this sweep.

4.2 Alternative design: order up to a learned target

Equation (2) suggests a different structure: let the network choose only the percentile, and let a fixed formula turn it into an order. A small MonoKAN level network reads only the four economic inputs and outputs a percentile u = σ(f(p, c, h, k)). The target stock is a smoothed empirical quantile of all demand observed so far. With z(1) ≤ … ≤ z(n) the sorted observations and tj = (j − 1)/(n − 1) their positions,

St = g(u) · [ z(1) + ∑j=2n σ((u − tj)/τ) · (z(j) − z(j−1)) ],   qt = (St − It)+,
(5)

where the sigmoid weights select the order statistics around position u, the width τ is learned, and g(u) = exp(a·u + b) with a ≥ 0 is a learned gain that increases with the level. Every term moves the right way. The sigmoid weights are non-negative, so St is nondecreasing in every observed demand. St increases with u, which MonoKAN makes increase in price and penalty and decrease in purchase and holding cost. And the subtraction makes the order nonincreasing in stock for any level network. The certificate therefore only has to cover four inputs, which is exactly why this design is a useful contrast. The level network has two layers of 8 nodes and 7-interval splines, {{R.LEVEL_PARAMS|,}} parameters. The final policy is trained on 5,000 products with each product's profit weighted equally in the loss.

The same decoder can use three kinds of demand information, which matters for fair comparisons: one fixed estimate from the first 32 days, the last 32 days updated every period, or all demand seen so far (the final policy). Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.1) suggest such order-up-to structures, but warn that they are fragile: they only work when the optimal policy is known to be of this form.

4.3 Baselines and the matched protocol

Besides the omniscient policy, we use two classical rules that estimate the target from observed demand: the empirical quantile at the critical fractile, and a Gamma distribution fitted by the method of moments. The Gamma fit is told that demand is Gamma distributed, an advantage no learned policy has. A comparison is only fair if both sides see the same data, so each rule is evaluated with each of the three kinds of information:

  • first 32 days, never updated: how the paper defines its non-omniscient benchmarks;
  • last 32 days, updated daily: what a policy in daily use sees, and exactly the information of the main design;
  • all demand seen so far: the information of the final alternative policy.

Within each comparison, every arm shares the products, demand paths, seeds, horizon, simulator, reward and training budget; for MonoKAN against KAN it also shares the architecture and parameter count. Only the factor under study changes.

5.1 Without the certificate, the stock rule breaks

The KAN without the certificate orders more as stock rises on {{D['kan']['violation']|.1f}}% of products on average (Figure 2a). This happens in every training run, but how often varies widely: {{KS[0]|.1f}}%, {{KS[1]|.1f}}% and {{KS[2]|.1f}}% of products for the three seeds. The breaks occur at {{R.KAN_VIOLATING_STEPS|.1f}}% of all swept stock steps; most are small, but the largest is an extra order of about {{R.KAN_WORST_RISE|.0f}}% of a day's demand. Figure 2b shows a typical case. Over part of the stock range, the KAN's order goes up as stock goes up. The certified MonoKAN on the same product decreases everywhere, as it must. Across all seeds and products MonoKAN breaks the rule nowhere, because it cannot.

The risk depends on the model family. The paper's FCNN never broke the rule in any run, while the KAN did in every run. A practitioner cannot know in advance which kind of model and which training run they will get. The certificate removes that uncertainty: no input combination, seen or unseen, can produce a larger order from more stock. Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.3) instead add a soft penalty for violations to the training loss, which makes breaks less likely but cannot exclude them.

5.2 What the certificate costs

The certificate is not free here. Paired over the 300 products, MonoKAN earns {{-DP['mean']|.2f}} per period less than the identical KAN without it: the difference is {{DP['mean']|.2f}} (95% CI {{DP['ci'][0]|.2f}} to {{DP['ci'][1]|.2f}}; paired t-test and Wilcoxon test both p {{DP['p_t']}}). MonoKAN is ahead on only {{DP['ahead']}}% of products. The cost is about {{cost_share|.1f}}% of profit, and the regret rises from {{D['kan']['regret']|.2f}}% to {{D['monokan']['regret']|.2f}}%. In this design the certificate enforces 37 directions at once, including the stock rule that the unconstrained network breaks so often, and that restriction is what the profit pays for.

The FCNN's three-seed average of {{D['fcnn']['reward']|,.2f}} is misleading. One run (seed {{R.FCNN_COLLAPSED['seed']}}) collapsed to {{R.FCNN_COLLAPSED['reward']|,.2f}} ({{R.FCNN_COLLAPSED['regret']|.2f}}% regret), while the other two average {{R.FCNN_HEALTHY_REWARD|,.2f}}, slightly above MonoKAN. The FCNN is less reliable to train rather than simply worse.

5.3 What the certificate cannot fix

Monotonicity fixes the direction of a response, not its strength. An order-up-to policy lowers its order one unit for every unit of stock until it stops ordering, which gives {{-R.OMNISCIENT_SLOPE|.2f}} on our sweep. All three networks react far more weakly, between {{-D['fcnn']['slope']|.2f}} and {{-D['kan']['slope']|.2f}} (Figure 3), so they keep ordering although enough stock is on hand. This likely explains much of their high regret, and the certificate cannot address it. Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.2) name exactly this as one reason to set monotonic networks aside: they fix the sign of a reaction but not its size. Our results confirm the concern.

As a consequence, no direct-quantity network comes close to a classical rule that sees the same information. The empirical quantile and the Gamma fit on the last 32 days reach {{C['percentile']['rolling'][1]|.2f}}% and {{C['gamma']['rolling'][1]|.2f}}% regret, against {{D['kan']['regret']|.2f}}% to {{D['fcnn']['regret']|.2f}}% for the networks. The paper's interpretability test points the same way. A direct-quantity policy's order at zero stock is its implied order-up-to level. Per unit of mean demand, which removes product scale, MonoKAN's implied level correlates only {{R.DIRECT_IMPLIED_CORR|.2f}} with the optimal level, against {{R.CLASSICAL_IMPLIED_CORR|.2f}} for the Gamma fit on the same history. In raw units every policy looks good, because product scale alone explains most of the variation. The certificate makes the direction of every input transparent, but the learned levels are only loosely tied to the optimum.

6 Results: Alternative Design

In short. With the stock rule built into the structure, the final policy reaches {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret with {{R.LEVEL_PARAMS|,}} parameters and no rule violations. In every information window it has a lower regret than the same KAN without the certificate. Against classical rules with the same information it is competitive, but not better.

6.1 From {{R.DEVELOPMENT[0][1]|.1f}}% to {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret

The first MonoKAN in this design, a deep network that output the target stock directly, reached {{R.DEVELOPMENT[0][1]|.2f}}% regret, worse than an unconstrained FCNN in the same structure ({{R.FIRST_FCNN_REGRET|.2f}}%) and twelve times worse than a classical rule. The same network without the certificate was just as poor, so the constraint was not the cause. Figure 4 shows the steps that fixed it, each applied cumulatively, with every step keeping the certificate. The two largest gains came from placing the spline knots where the data lies (Section 3.3) and from the soft-quantile structure of equation (5). Using all demand seen so far, weighting every product equally in the loss and training on more products brought the regret to {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}%. On 3,000 validation products the final policy reaches {{R.FINAL_REGRET_3000|.2f}}%.

Several plausible changes did not help (Appendix C). Making the KAN larger made it worse. A warm start that imitated the classical rule before profit training was severely harmful, because the static rule it imitated differs from the expanding-window policy that is deployed. A longer training horizon did not help either.

Why is the certificate free here, or even helpful? The subtraction already guarantees the stock rule, so the certificate only asks the level network to follow the four economic directions, which a good policy follows anyway. Our reading is that with ample data the unconstrained KAN finds these directions by itself, while with less data the constraint acts as useful prior knowledge. We have not tested this explanation directly. Two earlier controls point the same way. A parameter-matched MLP level network ties with MonoKAN ({{R.MLP_CONTROL['mean']|+.2f}}, p = {{R.MLP_CONTROL['p_t']}}). In a block with a running-mean decoder, MonoKAN leads the same KAN by {{R.EARLIER_KAN_CONTROL['mean']|+.2f}} (p = {{R.EARLIER_KAN_CONTROL['p_t']}}).

6.4 Interpretability, speed and data needs

With only four inputs the level network is easy to read (Figure 6a). The learned target stock rises with price and penalty and falls with purchase and holding cost, as certified. More strikingly, the learned percentile u matches the newsvendor fractile β of equation (2) with correlation {{R.NEWSVENDOR_CORR|.3f}} (Figure 6b). The fractile was never given to the model as an input, an initial value or a training target. The network recovered the textbook solution from profit alone.

The final policy has {{R.LEVEL_PARAMS|,}} parameters, against 15,297 for the first design. It is still slower per decision than a feed-forward network of the same size ({{R.TS_MS['monokan']|.2f}} ms against {{R.TS_MS['mlp']|.2f}} ms on a CPU), because spline evaluation is more expensive than a matrix product. For daily ordering decisions this does not matter. MonoKAN also does not need less data. With 100 training products it is worse than a parameter-matched MLP (Figure 7). What the certificate buys is reliable improvement as data grows: MonoKAN improves steadily, while the MLP degrades beyond 300 products.

The pattern is consistent. The certificate costs profit exactly where it does real work. In the main design it alone enforces the stock rule, and the unconstrained network breaks that rule on a third of the products. Where the structure already does the work, the certificate is free, and in the smaller setup it even helps. With the same data, the alternative's regret is a fraction of the main design's. The gap therefore comes from the structure, not from the information. Building inventory knowledge into the policy helped more than anything else we tried; with all demand seen so far, the matched alternative reaches {{R.MATCHED_EXPANDING_REGRET|.2f}}%.

Why then is the alternative not the main design? Its advantage rests entirely on knowing the shape of the answer in advance. In our setting the optimal policy is order-up-to (equation 2), so a structure that can only produce order-up-to policies loses nothing. Maggiar et al. (Section 5.3.1) call this route fragile, and their own experiments show why. With delivery lead times of 2 to 7 periods, where order-up-to is no longer optimal, order-up-to policies fall {{R.PAPER_LEAD_TIME_GAP[0]|.2f}}% to {{R.PAPER_LEAD_TIME_GAP[1]|.2f}}% behind a network that decides the quantity (their Table 4). The direct-quantity design assumes nothing about the shape of the optimal policy. It is the general method, and the one in which a certified guarantee is genuinely needed.

8.2 Limitations

  • A simple setting. Demand is stationary, orders arrive immediately and products are synthetic. This is exactly the setting in which order-up-to is optimal, and it favours the alternative design. We have not tested changing demand, lead times or real data.
  • Scale. We train on 1,000 to 5,000 products, compared with 40,000 in the paper, and use three seeds per experiment. Our absolute profit levels are therefore not comparable with the paper's. The paired comparisons, which are what our conclusions rest on, are not affected.
  • Checkpoint selection. The best checkpoint is selected on the same 300 validation products that we report on, which may flatter all learned policies slightly. The final alternative policy keeps its standing on 3,000 products ({{R.FINAL_REGRET_3000|.2f}}%), most of them unseen during selection (Appendix C).
  • The weak stock reaction is unexplained. All direct-quantity networks react to stock about half as strongly as they should. A plausible cause is that the stock levels visited during training cover only a narrow range; we have not verified this.
  • Model family. The stock-rule breaks are a property of the KAN family in our runs. The paper's FCNN did not break the rule, so how often an unconstrained model breaks it depends on the model.
  • The main design's training setup has not yet received the improvements that helped the alternative (5,000 products, per-product weighting), so its absolute performance is likely understated.

9 Conclusion

We asked what a certified monotonicity guarantee costs in an end-to-end inventory policy and where it matters. When the network decides the order itself, the guarantee does real work. Without it, the same network orders more as stock rises on a third of the products, in every training run. With MonoKAN this is impossible for any input, at a price of about {{cost_share|.1f}}% of profit. When the structure of the policy already enforces the stock rule, the guarantee is free, and in a smaller setup it even helps. The cost of the guarantee scales with the work it has to do. Building inventory knowledge into the structure produced the best policy of the project, at {{R.FINAL_REGRET|.2f}}% regret, but only because the shape of the optimal policy was known in advance. The guarantee fixes the direction of every response but not its strength, and in this simple setting a well-chosen classical rule with the same information remains better than both designs.

Three directions follow. First, the main design should receive the improvements that helped the alternative: more training products and per-product weighting. Second, a second property can be certified: the order should fall by at most one unit per unit of stock. Together with monotonicity, this bounds the stock reaction between 0 and −1 and addresses the weakness the current guarantee cannot fix. Third, settings with changing demand and lead times, where order-up-to policies are no longer optimal, are where a certified direct-quantity policy should be tested next.
